# 80166 RF Amplifier

## What the RF amplifier is intended to do

The 80166 assembly is the Triton 540 receiver's tunable RF amplifier. Its job
is not simply to make every antenna signal larger. It first favors the part of
the amateur band selected by the BAND and RESONATE controls, rejects an
especially troublesome signal at the receiver's 9 MHz intermediate frequency,
and then adds gain before passing the selected signal to the following mixer.

Ten-Tec describes it as a “single stage, dual gate MOSFET” amplifier with tuned
input and output coils.

### How the RESONATE rack tunes the board

L1 and L2 contain movable magnetic slugs. A slug increases the magnetic
permeability inside its coil and therefore changes the coil's inductance. The
RESONATE knob moves both slugs with a mechanical rack:

- Moving a slug farther into its coil increases inductance and lowers the
  resonant frequency.
- Withdrawing it decreases inductance and raises the resonant frequency.

This follows the familiar relationship, read as
“frequency equals one divided by 2π times the square root of LC”:

$$
f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}
$$

In plain text, this is **f₀ = 1/(2π√(LC))**, where $L$ is coil inductance
and $C$ is the effective tuning capacitance.
The BAND switch makes the large step from one amateur band to another by
changing capacitance. The RESONATE rack then provides continuous tuning within
that band by changing both inductances. L1 and L2 have separate slugs, but the
rack moves them together. During alignment their relative positions are
adjusted so that both circuits peak at the same dial setting.

The reconstructed bandswitch selects these coarse tuning components:

| `S4_POS` | Receiver band | L1-side fixed capacitor | L2-side adjustment |
|---:|---:|---:|:--|
| 1 | 3.5 MHz | C12, 620 pF | C19 |
| 2 | 7 MHz | C11, 220 pF | C17 |
| 3 | 14 MHz | C10, 56 pF | C16 |
| 4 | 21 MHz | C9, 22 pF | C15 |
| 5 | 28 MHz | C8, 10 pF | C13 in series with C14 |

These are component values, not the complete effective capacitance. Coil
self-capacitance, C66-3, switch and wiring capacitance, Q1, and the winding
taps also affect the measured peak. The LC equation therefore supplies a
starting estimate and direction of adjustment; the final model values come
from the simulated response.

### Why the amplifier has two tuned circuits

L1 tunes the signal **before** Q1, and L2 tunes it again **after** Q1. They have
different jobs:

- **L1 is the input preselector.** It favors the wanted frequency before it
  reaches Q1. This reduces out-of-band noise and strong unwanted signals that
  could overload the transistor.
- **L2 is Q1's tuned drain load and output selector.** A tuned circuit presents
  a useful RF load to the transistor near resonance, converting Q1's changing
  drain current into an RF voltage. Its output tap then couples the selected
  signal to the following mixer while rejecting much of Q1's broadband output.

Using a tuned circuit on both sides gives better rejection than putting one
tuned circuit on only one side of the transistor. It also lets L1 and L2 use
different taps for the impedances presented by the antenna, Q1, and the mixer.
The price is that the two peaks must track. If L1 is tuned to one frequency and
L2 to another, the board loses gain even though each individual circuit may
still resonate normally.

### What Q1 is expected to contribute

Q1 does the active amplification. A small RF-voltage change at gate 1 produces
a larger change in drain current. L2 turns that changing current into an RF
output voltage. A useful first approximation is:

$$
A_v \approx g_m Z_\mathrm{drain}
$$

where $g_m$ is Q1's transconductance and $Z_\mathrm{drain}$ is the effective
impedance of the resonant L2 circuit. The winding taps and mixer loading then
determine how much of that drain voltage appears at OUT.

The Ten-Tec manual does **not** give a normal 80166 gain figure. Based on the
nominal transconductance of the empirical 40823 model and the expected loading
of a tapped tuned circuit, an overall tuned-stage gain on the order of
**10–20 dB** is a reasonable engineering expectation. This is an inferred
range with low confidence, not a Ten-Tec specification or a completed
simulation result. Absolute gain depends strongly on the undocumented coil Q,
winding-tap ratios, actual 40823, and input impedance of the following mixer.
The lightly loaded AC model later in this document can produce much larger
voltage ratios—more than 70 dB at some very sharp modeled peaks. Those figures
include resonant voltage step-up and must not be treated as the real radio's
loaded power gain. An output-load and coil-loss sensitivity test is still
required before using the model to predict absolute gain.

### Why DEFEAT reduces the gain by about 25 dB

Gate 2 of Q1 acts as a gain-control electrode. In normal reception it is held
positive so that Q1 can amplify the signal selected by L1. When the crystal
calibrator is energized, it pulls DEFEAT low. The manual says this reduces the
stage gain by about 25 dB, a voltage ratio of approximately 18:1.

The purpose is not to mute the receiver. It suppresses the normal
antenna-to-mixer path while the calibrator is being used. Otherwise antenna
noise, nearby stations, and strong signals amplified by Q1 could compete with
or mask the small calibration marker. Reducing Q1's gain also helps prevent a
strong antenna signal from overloading the downstream mixer while the operator
is trying to hear the marker.

### What “loosely coupled” means

The calibrator does not drive Q1 through an ordinary wired connection. Its
output wire is placed close to an insulated wire connected to Q1's drain. The
two conductors form a very small capacitor—the “gimmick capacitance”—so only a
small amount of RF crosses the insulation. There is no direct DC connection.
The manual does not state its capacitance; the 1 pF used in the simulation is
an explicitly estimated starting value.

Loose coupling is intentional. A larger capacitor would inject a stronger
marker, but it would also load and detune L2, lower the tuned circuit's Q, and
could drive the following mixer too strongly. The tiny coupling leaves the
normal receiver response nearly undisturbed. The 25 dB DEFEAT action makes that
small injected marker easy to hear by suppressing the competing amplified
antenna signal. It is the combination of loose drain injection and reduced Q1
gain that lets the calibrator work without becoming a major load on the RF
amplifier.

The board also contains T1 and C7, a sharply tuned 9 MHz trap in the antenna
path. This reduces signals at the receiver's IF frequency before they reach
the transistor.

These functions are documented in the *Triton IV Model 540 Owner's Manual*,
PDF page 24 / printed page 3-8, under “80166 R.F. Amplifier.” The board
schematic and transistor-voltage table are on PDF page 25 / printed page 3-9.
The off-board bandswitch capacitors appear on PDF page 21 / printed page 3-4.

## High-level circuit operation

The signal path can be summarized as:

```text
ANT → 9 MHz trap → tuned input coil L1 → Q1 → tuned output coil L2 → OUT
```

1. **The antenna signal enters through C1.** C1 couples RF into the board while
   blocking any DC connection to the antenna.

2. **T1 and C7 reject 9 MHz.** T1 is inserted in the antenna path and is coupled
   to the adjustable capacitor C7. At the trap frequency, the coupled resonant
   circuit makes the series signal path strongly attenuate 9 MHz before it
   reaches L1. The manual emphasizes that the resulting null is “very sharp”
   (PDF p. 24 / printed p. 3-8, “9 MHz Trap Adjustment,” step 2).

3. **L1 selects the wanted input frequency.** The bandswitch connects the
   appropriate fixed capacitor across L1. Turning RESONATE moves L1's slug and
   changes its inductance. The antenna is connected to a tap on L1, while
   another tap feeds Q1 through C2. The taps couple the signal without placing
   the full antenna and transistor loading directly across the complete tuned
   winding. C2 passes RF but isolates L1 from Q1's gate-1 DC bias.

4. **Q1 supplies controlled RF gain.** Q1 is an RCA 40823 dual-gate,
   N-channel depletion MOSFET. Gate 1 receives the selected RF signal and is
   DC-biased by R3 and R4. The source voltage developed across R6 provides
   self-bias. Gate 2 receives the external DEFEAT control voltage, allowing the
   radio to reduce the stage's gain without disturbing the tuned circuits.

5. **L2 selects and couples the amplified signal to OUT.** Q1's drain connects
   to a tap on L2. The bandswitch selects the matching output trimmer, while the
   RESONATE mechanism moves L2's slug together with L1's. The OUT connection
   uses another L2 tap to feed the following mixer. At DC, current reaches Q1's
   drain through R5 and the continuous L2 winding. At RF, C6 holds L2's supply
   end close to ground, so that same winding acts as Q1's resonant drain load.
   R5 and C6 also keep amplified RF out of the receiver's supply wiring.

For proper operation, the resonant peaks of L1 and L2 must coincide. If they do
not track, one circuit can reject a frequency that the other is trying to pass.
That is why the manual first establishes L1/L2 tracking at both ends of the
3.5 MHz band—3.5 and 4.0 MHz—and then adjusts the individual output trimmers
on the higher bands.

## Simulation results

The simulations use the reconstructed KiCad schematic and the project-local
empirical RCA 40823 model. The transistor model is suitable for checking bias
and gain-control trends, but it is not a factory RCA model. Accordingly, the
manual values are treated as the historical reference and the simulation
results are not presented as measurements of an original board.

### DC operating-point check

The DC check answers two basic questions:

1. Does Q1 receive approximately the bias shown in the service manual during
   normal reception?
2. Does the stage turn off when its receive supply and DEFEAT voltage are
   removed?

The simulation was run at 27 °C. A simulation-only 1 GΩ resistor was connected
from the otherwise floating C13-C14 junction to ground. It provides a DC
reference for the SPICE operating-point solver and is far too large to load
the RF circuit meaningfully.

#### Normal receive

The receive run used `/R = 12 V` and `/DEFEAT = 3.9 V`. The manual's voltage
table specifies RF CONTROL fully clockwise with the rack plate removed
(PDF p. 25 / printed p. 3-9).

| Q1 terminal | Manual | Simulation | Difference | Assessment |
|:--|--:|--:|--:|:--|
| Pin 1 — Drain | 11.4 V | 11.552 V | +0.152 V (+1.3%) | Very close |
| Pin 2 — Gate 2 | 3.9 V | 3.900 V | 0.000 V (0.0%) | Exact applied control voltage |
| Pin 3 — Gate 1 | 1.1 V | 1.051 V | −0.049 V (−4.5%) | Close |
| Pin 4 — Source | 1.6 V | 2.071 V | +0.471 V (+29.5%) | Model conducts more current |

The drain and two gate voltages agree closely with the service data. Gate 1 is
set primarily by the R4-R3 voltage divider. Using the simulated 11.556 V supply
node gives:

$$
V_{G1}
= 11.556
  \left(\frac{47\ \mathrm{k\Omega}}
  {470\ \mathrm{k\Omega}+47\ \mathrm{k\Omega}}\right)
= 1.051\ \mathrm{V}
$$

The main disagreement is the source voltage. With R6 equal to 470 Ω, the
simulation implies:

$$
I_S
= \frac{2.071\ \mathrm{V}}{470\ \Omega}
= 4.41\ \mathrm{mA}
$$

The manual's 1.6 V source reading would imply approximately 3.40 mA. Thus the
empirical model conducts about 30% more current at this self-biased operating
point than the particular condition represented by the manual. Early
dual-gate MOSFETs had substantial device-to-device spread, so this difference
does not by itself indicate an error in the reconstructed resistor network.
It does mean that absolute gain predictions must be treated cautiously.

The simulated current drawn from `/R` was 4.441 mA. At DC, OUT also sits at
approximately the drain voltage because OUT is a tap on the continuous L2
winding. This DC voltage does not mean that DC is delivered through the
receiver signal chain; subsequent coupling circuitry blocks it as appropriate.

#### Transmit/off condition

The second run used `/R = 0 V` and `/DEFEAT = 0 V`.

| Q1 terminal | Manual | Simulation | Assessment |
|:--|--:|--:|:--|
| Pin 1 — Drain | 0.05 V | ≈0 V | Ideal simulated supply removes the residual voltage |
| Pin 2 — Gate 2 | 0 V | ≈0 V | Matches |
| Pin 3 — Gate 1 | 0 V | ≈0 V | Matches |
| Pin 4 — Source | 0 V | ≈0 V | Matches |
| `/R` supply current | Not specified | ≈0 A | Stage is off |

The tiny calculated values in the femtovolt and femtoampere range are numerical
roundoff and are physically zero. The manual's 0.05 V drain reading is
consistent with a small residual voltage in the complete radio; the simulation
uses an ideal zero-volt source and therefore removes it completely.

#### DC-check conclusion

The reconstructed bias network behaves correctly in both operating states.
Normal-receive drain and gate voltages closely reproduce the service data, and
the stage shuts down when the receive and DEFEAT supplies are removed. The
source-voltage difference is retained as an explicit limitation of the
empirical 40823 model rather than hidden by adjusting the circuit to force a
match.

### Normal operation at 3.5 MHz

This study follows one small signal through the aligned board and also shows
what the RESONATE control does to it. The setup uses the manual-aligned 3.5 MHz
state: `S4_POS=1`, `L_RACK=17.2 µH`, C19 = 36 pF, `/R=12 V`, and
`/DEFEAT=3.9 V`. These are the normal-receive supply conditions, not the
DEFEAT or transmit condition.

#### RESONATE puts the passband on the signal

The first plot compares three positions of the ganged L1/L2 rack. Increasing
the rack inductance moves the response to a lower frequency; decreasing it
moves the response higher. This is the operating meaning of turning
RESONATE: the operator moves both tuned circuits until their common passband
is centered on the station.

![Changing the RESONATE rack moves the 3.5 MHz passband](spice/studies/80166/operation-3p5mhz/figures/rack-tuning.png)

At the fitted 17.2 µH setting, the simulated response peaks at 3.5012 MHz and
has 20.189 dB voltage gain at exactly 3.500 MHz. The sampled rack sweep found
20.240 dB at 17.0 µH, only 0.052 dB more. That tiny difference is below the
precision justified by the provisional coil-loss and output-load models. The
important result is that turning the rack away from this region moves the
passband away from 3.5 MHz and reduces the received signal.

#### The signal through L1, Q1, and L2

For the time-domain run, a 3.5 MHz, 10 µV-peak sine generator drives the board
through 50 Ω. That 10 µV is the generator's open-circuit setting. Loading by
the tuned input network leaves 0.927 µV peak at the board's `ANT` node. The
table therefore references every gain to the signal actually entering the
board at `ANT`, not to the unloaded generator setting.

| Circuit point | What is happening there | RF peak | Ratio from `ANT` | Gain from `ANT` |
|:--|:--|--:|--:|--:|
| `ANT` | Signal at the board input | 0.927 µV | 1.000× | 0.000 dB |
| Q1 gate 1 | L1 has selected and transformed the signal | 2.459 µV | 2.654× | 8.477 dB |
| Q1 drain | Q1 is working into L2's tuned drain load | 5.669 µV | 6.119× | 15.733 dB |
| `OUT` | L2 has coupled the selected signal to the output tap | 9.469 µV | 10.220× | 20.189 dB |

The overall voltage ratio is:

$$
A_v
= \frac{V_\mathrm{OUT}}{V_\mathrm{ANT}}
= \frac{9.469\ \mu\mathrm{V}}{0.927\ \mu\mathrm{V}}
= 10.220
$$

and its decibel equivalent is:

$$
G_v
= 20\log_{10}(A_v)
= 20\log_{10}(10.220)
= 20.189\ \mathrm{dB}.
$$

The 8.477 dB rise from `ANT` to gate 1 is **not Q1 gain**. It is resonant
voltage transformation in the tapped L1 input network. The gate-1-to-drain
increase is 7.256 dB; that is Q1 acting together with L2's resonant drain
load. The remaining drain-to-`OUT` increase is 4.456 dB from the tapped L2
output network in this model:

$$
8.477\ \mathrm{dB}
+ 7.256\ \mathrm{dB}
+ 4.456\ \mathrm{dB}
= 20.189\ \mathrm{dB}.
$$

![Small 3.5 MHz signal building up and passing through the aligned RF amplifier](spice/studies/80166/operation-3p5mhz/figures/signal-walk.png)

The upper panel shows the resonant response building after the sine source is
switched on; it reaches steady state in roughly 10 µs in this model. The lower
four panels show the final six RF cycles. Their DC bias has been subtracted so
the microvolt RF can be seen: gate 1 normally sits near 1.05 V, while the
drain and OUT sit near 11.55 V.

This simulation demonstrates the intended sequence:

```text
small antenna signal
    → L1 selects it
    → Q1 supplies active gain
    → L2 selects and transforms the amplified signal
    → larger selected signal at OUT
```

It does not establish the original radio's absolute power gain. The empirical
40823 model, estimated coil Q and tap ratios, and provisional `1 MΩ || 5 pF`
OUT load all affect the numerical voltage ratio. The retained CSV data,
settings, limitations, and reproduction command are in the
[3.5 MHz operation-study README](spice/studies/80166/operation-3p5mhz/README.md)
and [manifest](spice/studies/80166/operation-3p5mhz/manifest.csv).

### AC alignment: simulation of the manual procedure

The AC runs follow the manual's complete procedure. The relative L1/L2 slug
relationship is established at 3.5 and 4.0 MHz. The higher-band steps move the
ganged rack to peak L1 and then use only the selected L2 trimmer. The manual
explicitly says, “No further adjustment of L2 is necessary” after the low-band
tracking procedure (PDF p. 24 / printed p. 3-8, step 7).

All runs used `/R=12 V`, `/DEFEAT=3.9 V`, an `AC 1` source through 50 Ω at
ANT, and the provisional `1 MΩ || 5 pF` simulation load at OUT. The plotted
quantity is the voltage ratio `OUT / ANT`; `Q1_GATE1 / ANT` is also retained
in every CSV so the input circuit can be inspected separately.
The retained files, run settings, and provenance are indexed in the
[`manifest.csv`](spice/studies/80166/manual-alignment/manifest.csv).

#### What the temporary 0.01 µF capacitor does

For the first half of each band adjustment, the manual connects a temporary
0.01 µF capacitor from that band's L2-trimmer terminal to chassis. At RF,
0.01 µF is much larger than the 5–620 pF tuning capacitors and behaves almost
like a short circuit. It suppresses L2's normal resonance, so the observed
peak is dominated by L1. In the schematic this temporary part is C99.

![Placement of the temporary 0.01 µF L2-bypass capacitor for the 3.5 MHz alignment step](spice/studies/80166/manual-alignment/fixtures/3p5_l2-bypass-placement.png)

The image shows the exact simulation connection: C99 goes from the selected
3.5 MHz contact on S4B to chassis ground. It is not connected across L1, Q1,
or the antenna input.

With L2 suppressed, the RESONATE rack is adjusted for maximum response at the
generator frequency. That establishes the necessary L1 inductance for the
selected input capacitor. C99 is then removed, restoring L2, and the output
side is peaked at the same frequency.

#### Why the 3.5 and 4.0 MHz steps set the tracking

The 3.5 MHz bandswitch position covers a range rather than just one spot.
Alternating the manual's 3.5 and 4.0 MHz adjustments determines two things:

1. The 3.5 MHz output trimmer sets the L2 capacitance near the low end.
2. The physical L2 slug adjustment sets how L2 follows L1 toward the high end.

Those are the two degrees of freedom needed for two-point tracking. Repeating
the endpoints until neither can be improved makes the two tuned circuits
follow one another through the rack's travel. The manual calls this the
procedure that “tracks L2 to L1” (PDF p. 24 / printed p. 3-8, step 6).

The empirical magnetics model required this fitted relationship:

$$
L_1 = L_\mathrm{RACK}
$$

$$
L_2
= 1.97963\ \mu\mathrm{H}
  + 0.0849053L_\mathrm{RACK}
$$

This is a compact way to reproduce the simulated endpoint tracking. It is not
a measurement of either Ten-Tec coil, and its small L2 slope must not be read
as the physical slug-motion ratio. It absorbs undocumented winding geometry,
tap positions, core response, loading, and parasitic capacitance into two
fitted numbers.

#### Why the higher bands need only trimmer adjustment

Once the relative L2 slug position is set, moving the rack changes both coils
together. Each higher bandswitch position already has a fixed capacitor for
L1 and its own independent trimmer for L2. The sequence is therefore:

1. Install C99 and move the rack until L1 peaks at the specified frequency.
2. Remove C99 without changing the rack.
3. Adjust only that band's L2 trimmer for maximum output.

Changing a higher-band L2 trimmer corrects that band without disturbing the
mechanical L1/L2 relationship established on 3.5–4.0 MHz. This is the logic
behind the manual's direction to align 14.200, 21.200, and 29.000 MHz by the
same procedure (PDF p. 24 / printed p. 3-8, step 8).

#### The math used during the simulated alignment

The resonance equation can be rearranged to estimate either inductance or
capacitance:

$$
L = \frac{1}{(2\pi f_0)^2 C}
  = \frac{1}{4\pi^2 f_0^2 C}
$$

$$
C = \frac{1}{(2\pi f_0)^2 L}
  = \frac{1}{4\pi^2 f_0^2 L}
$$

In plain text: **L = 1/(4π²f₀²C)** and **C = 1/(4π²f₀²L)**. These forms
were useful for checking the scale of a trial value, but the complete circuit
is not a single ideal inductor and capacitor. Its taps, transistor loading,
model loss, switch capacitance, and coil parasitics shift the result.

The fastest rack correction came from comparing the simulated peak with the
target. If capacitance is unchanged, inductance scales with the square of the
frequency ratio:

$$
L_\mathrm{new}
= L_\mathrm{old}
  \left(\frac{f_\mathrm{old}}{f_\mathrm{target}}\right)^2
$$

For the first 29 MHz rack trial, `L_RACK = 1.61 µH` produced an L1-dominated
peak at 27.68365 MHz. The next estimate was therefore:

$$
\begin{aligned}
L_\mathrm{new}
&= 1.61\ \mu\mathrm{H}
   \left(\frac{27.68365}{29.00000}\right)^2 \\
&= 1.4672\ \mu\mathrm{H}
\end{aligned}
$$

The next run used 1.47 µH and peaked at 28.65478 MHz. Repeating the same
calculation gave 1.4352 µH, which led to the 1.435 µH trial. Successive
corrections converged on the retained `L_RACK = 1.4253 µH` result.

The same square-law relationship applies to capacitance when inductance is
held fixed:

$$
C_\mathrm{new}
= C_\mathrm{old}
  \left(\frac{f_\mathrm{old}}{f_\mathrm{target}}\right)^2
$$

This predicts the direction and approximate size of a trimmer change.
However, the 28 MHz output network requires an additional calculation because
C13 and C14 are in series:

$$
C_\mathrm{eq}
= \frac{C_{13}C_{14}}{C_{13}+C_{14}}
$$

Solving that equation for the adjustable C13 gives:

$$
C_{13}
= \frac{C_\mathrm{eq}C_{14}}{C_{14}-C_\mathrm{eq}}
$$

With the initial C13 = 24 pF and C14 = 30 pF, their ideal series value was
13.33 pF. Scaling the 23.19690 MHz baseline peak toward 29.000 MHz predicted
an effective series value of approximately 8.53 pF:

$$
C_{\mathrm{eq,new}}
= 13.33\ \mathrm{pF}
  \left(\frac{23.19690}{29.00000}\right)^2
= 8.53\ \mathrm{pF}
$$

That corresponds to an idealized C13 estimate of about 11.9 pF. It was only a
starting estimate: the complete L2 model contains its own 6 pF capacitance,
winding taps, losses, Q1 drain loading, and OUT loading. Consequently, the
actual sweep required a much smaller C13 setting.

Once two nearby trials bracketed the target, linear interpolation was more
efficient than another large LC estimate:

$$
x_\mathrm{new}
= x_2
  + (f_\mathrm{target}-f_2)
    \left(\frac{x_2-x_1}{f_2-f_1}\right)
$$

Here $x$ is the parameter being adjusted. For example, C13 = 6.13 pF gave
28.94815 MHz and C13 = 5.95 pF gave 28.98317 MHz. Substitution predicted
approximately 5.8635 pF, so the next run used 5.865 pF. A final interpolation
between the 5.865 pF and 5.810 pF runs produced approximately 5.787 pF.

The temporary 0.01 µF capacitor can also be checked mathematically. Its
capacitive reactance is:

$$
X_C = \frac{1}{2\pi fC}
$$

For C99 = 0.01 µF, $X_C$ is approximately 4.55 Ω at 3.5 MHz and 0.55 Ω at
29 MHz. That low impedance is why connecting C99 from the selected S4B
terminal to chassis suppresses the L2 resonance during the L1 adjustment.

Finally, the table below uses the CSV samples to calculate frequency error
and loss at the exact manual target:

$$
\Delta f = f_\mathrm{peak}-f_\mathrm{target}
$$

$$
\mathrm{loss\ at\ target}
= G_\mathrm{peak}-G(f_\mathrm{target})
$$

#### The final 29 MHz adjustment

The 28 MHz bandswitch position is aligned at **29.000 MHz**, not 28.000 MHz.
With C99 installed, reducing `L_RACK` to 1.4253 µH put the L1-dominated peak at
28.99429 MHz. After removing C99, the initial C13 = 24 pF setting placed the
full-stage peak at only 23.19690 MHz. Reducing C13 raised the L2 resonance:
8.07 pF gave 27.94785 MHz, 6.13 pF gave 28.94815 MHz, and 5.787 pF gave
28.999365 MHz.

This position is slightly different from the other output ranges: C13 is in
series with the fixed 30 pF C14. At the final setting their ideal series
capacitance is

$$
C_\mathrm{series}
= \frac{(5.787)(30)}{5.787+30}
= 4.85\ \mathrm{pF}
$$

before switch, wiring, and coil parasitics are added. Turning C13 therefore
changes the smaller effective capacitance seen by the 28 MHz L2 circuit; it
does not place 5.787 pF directly across the entire winding.

![The 29 MHz rack adjustment and C13 convergence](spice/studies/80166/manual-alignment/figures/29mhz-adjustment.png)

The upper panel shows the L1-dominated response with C99 installed, followed
by selected full-stage C13 trials after C99 was removed. The lower panel is
made from the C13 iteration CSVs and plots the resulting `OUT/ANT` peak against
C13. It shows the peak approaching 29.000 MHz as C13 is reduced.

This is a direct illustration of **f₀ = 1/(2π√(LC))**. Once L1 is fixed by
the rack, decreasing the selected L2 capacitance raises L2's resonant
frequency. The final response is only 635 Hz below the manual target in the
sampled sweep.

#### What is documented and what is fitted

- **Documented:** the alignment order, frequencies, temporary 0.01 µF
  connection, no further L2 slug adjustment after low-band tracking, input
  fixed capacitors, and 5–60 pF output-trimmer range.
- **Calculated from exported CSV data:** the peak frequencies, target errors,
  and loss at each manual target in the table.
- **Fitted simulation parameters:** `L_RACK`, the L2 tracking equation, and the
  numerical trimmer settings.
- **Inferred:** approximately 8 pF of parasitic capacitance on the 7 MHz output
  circuit.
- **Still unknown:** actual L1/L2 inductance versus rack position, coil Q,
  exact tap ratios, real mixer loading, and trustworthy absolute stage gain.

The very high and very narrow peaks in the lightly loaded model are useful for
finding resonance but not for predicting received-signal gain. Coil loss,
the following mixer, wiring, and the actual 40823 will lower and broaden the
response. A load-and-loss sensitivity sweep is the next step before making an
absolute gain claim.

#### Reproducing the final run

The schematic is saved in the final 29 MHz state: `S4_POS=5`,
`L_RACK=1.4253u`, C13 = 5.787 pF, C14 = 30 pF, and C99 excluded from
simulation. The project script
[`spice/tools/run_80166_headless.py`](spice/tools/run_80166_headless.py) starts from a
fresh KiCad-exported SPICE netlist and runs ngspice 46 in KiCad-compatible
`ps lt a` mode. A fresh export of the saved schematic reproduced the same
28.999365 MHz peak as the final CSV.
